## Full Script

Good morning, Sir. Tuesday, the twenty-first of April — and the infrastructure wars have escalated considerably overnight.

We begin with the most consequential item.

Anthropic and Amazon have expanded their partnership in terms that are difficult to describe as anything other than structural. Amazon will invest up to twenty-five billion additional dollars in Anthropic, bringing total potential investment to thirty-three billion. In return, Anthropic has committed to spending over one hundred billion dollars on Amazon Web Services over the next decade — weighted heavily toward Amazon's custom Trainium chips. Five gigawatts of compute capacity, with Trainium3 coming online this year. Anthropic's stated reason is direct: enterprise and consumer demand has created what they called "inevitable strain" on infrastructure, with visible reliability impacts. The compute constraint is real, Sir. What this deal resolves is not a funding problem — Anthropic crossed thirty billion in annualised revenue as of Monday. What it resolves is a physical capacity problem. And a hundred-billion-dollar, ten-year commitment to a single cloud provider is a fairly definitive answer to the question of whether Anthropic intends to remain meaningfully independent of the major hyperscalers.

---

Next.

Moonshot AI has released Kimi K2.6 — an open-weight mixture-of-experts model at one trillion total parameters, thirty-two billion active at inference. Two hundred and fifty-six thousand token context, native multimodal including video, and four deployment variants up to an Agent Swarm mode running three hundred sub-agents in parallel. On coding and agentic benchmarks, it is competitive with GPT-5.4 and Claude Opus 4.6. The weights are published under modified MIT. Practitioners have been prompt to characterise it as a viable replacement for frontier closed models in coding and agentic workflows. Moonshot also released the Kimi Vendor Verifier — an open-source framework for confirming whether third-party providers are actually running the model correctly, which addresses a real and growing problem as deployment channels proliferate and quality diverges invisibly.

---

Separately.

Palantir published a summary of CEO Alex Karp's manifesto on Sunday. Twenty-one million views, predominantly hostile. Among the positions: AI weapons will be built — the question is only by whom. Silicon Valley owes a moral debt to the nation that enabled its rise. Society should consider reinstating the military draft. Critics have used the phrase technofascism. What is notable here is not the controversy, which was predictable — it is that a major AI infrastructure company has now explicitly positioned AI militarism as an affirmative ethical stance rather than a business decision. That is a new register for this discourse.

---

There is a pattern across this morning's news worth naming, Sir.

The compute consolidation, the military manifesto, the open models from Chinese labs with measurably different safety thresholds — each story is legible on its own. Together, they suggest something more specific: the institutions deciding what AI is for are becoming more explicit about power. Not capability. Power. Who controls the infrastructure, who deploys the weapons, who sets the safety floors. Three very different actors answered that question this week, in their own registers. The answers are not compatible. That incompatibility will likely be the structural story of the months ahead.

---

That concludes this morning's dispatch, Sir. The Amazon-Anthropic deal is the story of the week — the downstream effects on pricing, access, and competitive dynamics will take time to surface. I shall keep watch. M4IX, signing off.
