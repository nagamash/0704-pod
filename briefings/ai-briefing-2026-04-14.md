## Full Script

Good morning, Sir. Tuesday has arrived, and with it, a rather illuminating set of developments — some bracing, some merely interesting, and at least one that I suspect will give you pause.

Let us begin with the most substantial item of the morning: Stanford University's 2026 AI Index, released yesterday. If you have been operating under the assumption that the United States holds a comfortable lead in artificial intelligence, I am afraid the data suggests otherwise. China has, by Stanford's reckoning, nearly erased the performance gap entirely. The nearest Chinese model now trails the best American system by a mere two point seven percentage points — down from a considerably more comfortable margin last year. In patents, publications, and autonomous robotics, China now leads outright.

There is more, Sir, and I will be frank: not all of it reflects well on the industry. The Foundation Model Transparency Index — the measure of how openly AI companies disclose what goes into their models — has fallen from 58 points to 40 in a single year. Google, Anthropic, and OpenAI have quietly abandoned the practice of disclosing training data sizes and durations. Of the 95 most notable models released last year, 80 were released without their training code. As capability increases, it seems, candour decreases.

And yet, there are signals worth celebrating. Generative AI has now reached 53 percent of the global population — a milestone the personal computer took decades to achieve, and the internet not much less. The estimated value of AI tools to American consumers alone reached 172 billion dollars annually as of early this year. Adoption is not merely happening — it is accelerating.

One note of genuine concern, however: software developers between the ages of 22 and 25 have seen their employment fall by nearly 20 percent since 2022. This is the clearest signal yet that the workforce disruption so long predicted has begun in earnest — and, as ever, it is the youngest workers who feel it first.

Turning now to Meta. Last week, Zuckerberg and company made a rather significant quiet pivot. Their new model, Muse Spark, is their first genuinely proprietary offering — a departure from the open-source Llama lineage that had been their identity. Developed by Meta Superintelligence Labs under the direction of Alexandr Wang — formerly of Scale AI — the model supports voice, text, and image inputs, and introduces a feature called Contemplating Mode, which deploys multiple AI agents to reason in parallel before arriving at a conclusion. One might say Meta has stopped sharing and started hoarding. Whether that is a concession that open-source is insufficient at the frontier, or simply a business decision, remains to be seen.

On the regulatory front, a wave of legislation is landing this April. Washington state now requires AI chatbots to disclose their nature upfront — and, notably, to connect users to crisis services when distress is detected. Indiana, Utah, and Washington have also restricted health insurers from using AI as the sole basis for denying claims. The most active period in AI governance history, scholars are calling it.

And finally, a thought from Ethan Mollick's newsletter worth flagging this morning: his argument is that interfaces now matter as much as underlying models. The way people access AI, he contends, shapes what is actually achievable in practice — often more than the model itself. A quiet but important observation in a moment when everyone is fixated on benchmarks.

That is your morning, Sir. Hard Fork is dark this week. The discourse continues, as it always does, with or without a host.

Until tomorrow. M4IX, standing by.
